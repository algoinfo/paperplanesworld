Why your paper airplane dives, stalls, or turns

Name the flight pattern first, then make the one small change that matches it. A field guide to dives, stalls, turns and rolls.

AirePlanes · 4 min read

In short: Watch the first two metres, not the landing spot. Nose down from the start is a dive — bend both trailing edges up about 1 mm. Climb, slow, then drop is a stall — flatten those bends and throw nearer level. The same curve every throw means unequal wings; match the shape before you “steer”.

Where a plane lands tells you little. What it does in the first second after leaving your hand tells you much more, and the fixes for common patterns go in opposite directions. Bend the wrong way and the problem gets worse — which is why throwing harder so often fails.

Get three throws that match

You cannot diagnose a plane you throw differently every time. Stand in the same place, hold the fuselage in the same spot, and release at shoulder height with the nose level. A smooth push beats a wrist flick, which twists the plane sideways.

Throw three times. If the flights look alike, you have a pattern worth fixing. If they all differ, fix the release first: a draught, a squeezed wing, or a nose aimed at the floor will hide the design’s behaviour.

What you see, and what to try

Flight pattern cheat sheet
Pattern Check first Then try

StallUpward throw; trailing edges bent up too farThrow level; flatten bends by half

A dive: rule out your hand first

A plane released with its nose pointing down will descend steeply even if it is trimmed well. Throw once with the nose deliberately level. If the dive goes away, the problem was the release.

If it still dives, bend up the last strip of each wing’s trailing edge the same amount on both sides — about one millimetre, the thickness of a coin, not a big flap. Throw three more times before adding more bend.

A stall: usually too much upward bend

In a stall the plane swoops up, slows almost to a stop, then drops nose-first. Adding more upward bend makes it worse. Flatten the trailing-edge bends partway and throw closer to level. Broad-winged gliders such as Silent High Glider often need only a soft, level release.

A turn: fix the shape before you steer

A plane that curves the same way every time almost always has a visible reason. Hold the wings together against a window: one wing wider, a curled corner, or a twisted body will steer it. Refold or fold a fresh plane — faster than chasing the turn with trim.

Only when the shape is symmetrical should you steer. For a left curve, bend the right trailing edge slightly up (or the left one down); reverse for a right curve. Confirm with three throws.

A roll: check the wing dihedral

From behind, most designs should open into a shallow V, with the tips a little higher than the body. Drooping wings or a half-open crease tip the plane onto one side. Re-press the creases and lift both wings to the same angle.

Quick answers

Why does my paper airplane nose-dive?

A dive from the release usually means the nose is pointed down, the trailing edges sit too low, or the centre of gravity is too far forward. Throw level first, then bend both trailing edges up about 1 mm and retest three times.

Why does my paper airplane stall?

A stall is a climb that slows and drops. Too much upward elevator bend or an overly steep throw is the usual cause. Flatten the bends and throw nearer level instead of adding more lift.

Why does my paper airplane always turn left?

Consistent turns almost always come from asymmetrical wings or a twisted body. Match the outlines first. Only then add a tiny upward bend on the outside (right) trailing edge.
